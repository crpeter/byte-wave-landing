A school should be able to say no to a chatbot without losing its search engine.

Los Angeles Unified has temporarily disabled Google's AI Overviews, according to the Associated Press. The district is pushing back while Google refuses to offer schools an AI-free version of Search.

Google's November 3 announcement promises new controls: Search and several AI products will be off for K–12 student accounts until administrators enable them. It also promotes Guided Learning, which Google says helps students work through problems instead of immediately supplying answers.

But where is the setting for a school that wants search results and does not want generative AI?

In his November 9 letter to Google, Senator Ed Markey describes the policy scheduled to begin early next year: schools that reject AI would lose Google Search on their student accounts. He asks Google to preserve search access when schools disable generative AI.

That timing matters. The announced restriction is still ahead. Los Angeles's workaround is already here.

Google can make its case for Guided Learning. Teachers can assess it. Schools can decide whether to use it. None of that requires making access to an established research tool conditional on accepting a different product.

We have already written about why students need to do the thinking themselves. Finding a source, reading past its opening paragraph, and noticing where two accounts disagree are part of learning. Schools deserve to protect that work.

A company does not acquire the right to set classroom policy because its products became convenient. And a school should not need a workaround to enforce a decision about what its students use.

For ByteWave, the principle is straightforward: useful software should leave people with meaningful control. That includes the ability to decline a feature the company is excited to sell.

Google should provide a supported way for schools to disable generative AI while keeping Search available.

If the product is good enough, let teachers choose it.

To Protect Your Face, First Upload Your Face.

By ByteWave · Published October 7, 2026

AI disclosure: This article includes text generated with an AI language model (LLM). Published by ByteWave, which makes the app discussed here.

You did not ask to be impersonated. Protecting yourself can still mean handing over more personal information.

YouTube’s experimental likeness-detection tool looks for videos that may use an enrolled creator’s face. Enrollment requires a government-issued ID and a video selfie. YouTube says verification helps prevent fraudulent use of the tool.

What are you agreeing to?

  • Face matching. YouTube uses the selfie and faces from your uploads to build a reference for finding possible impersonations.
  • Stored identity data. Its policy describes retaining your legal name, reference video, and likeness template for up to three years after your last sign-in, unless you withdraw consent or delete the account.
  • An optional extra. Allowing face and voice templates to improve likeness-detection models is a separate choice you can revoke.

Google says enrollment data is not used to train its generative AI models without consent. Turning off detection deletes the stored likeness information; ID information in your Payments Profile has a separate deletion control.

Detection still leaves you work.

You review matches and request removal. The tool can miss impersonations, and finding a match does not guarantee a takedown.

The protection can be valuable. So can asking why ordinary creators must spend their time finding, reviewing, and reporting fabricated versions of themselves.

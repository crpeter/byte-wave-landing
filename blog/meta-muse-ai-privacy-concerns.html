Your private messages should not become an AI feature by surprise. And when an assistant produces sensitive information, ‘it hallucinated’ should start an investigation, not end one.

What the reporting establishes

Reuters reported on November 18 that Meta employees raised concerns about unexpected Signal-message access and unsolicited outputs containing apparent bank balances. Meta said many suspected account mix-ups were hallucinations and had been addressed; it characterized the examples as isolated incidents. Reuters could not establish whether the financial information was leaked or invented.

Tests conducted with Signal and the AI Now Institute reproduced message access through Android notification permissions and macOS accessibility and screenshot tools. The same requests were blocked on an iPhone. The updated report says Apple’s subsequent macOS changes closed the tested pathway. These findings describe access through device permissions, not a demonstrated break in Signal’s encryption.

Permission needs to mean something

A broad setup permission is a poor substitute for a clear explanation of what an agent will read. Someone asking for help with one task should not have to anticipate every other use a company might find for that access. Meaningful consent should identify the information, the purpose, and the boundaries. Those boundaries should remain visible and easy to revoke.

‘It hallucinated’ is a claim that needs evidence

We should not accept the hallucination defense on trust. An AI system can fabricate information, but that possibility does not establish where a particular output came from. The company operating the system should explain how it investigated the incident and ruled out inappropriate access or information crossing between accounts. A label alone answers none of those questions.

Consider a hypothetical: an assistant displays a bank balance that turns out to match someone’s real account. ‘It just happened to guess correctly’ would demand scrutiny, not a shrug. A matching number alone would not prove a leak, but calling it a hallucination would not prove the absence of one either. Otherwise, any disclosure could be dismissed with an explanation users have no way to test.

That hypothetical is not an established fact about these reported balances. The uncertainty must remain explicit. It is a reason to demand evidence, not permission to declare either a confirmed breach or a clean bill of health.

What a credible explanation should include

A credible response should give users enough information to assess the conclusion while protecting the people involved:

  • What information and permissions were available to the agent when the output was produced?
  • What records were examined to distinguish fabricated content from retrieved data or a mix-up between accounts?
  • What was fixed, what remains uncertain, and how was the fix checked?

We are asking for an accountable explanation, not publication of someone else’s private records. Even a demonstrated fabrication leaves a reliability problem: users should not have to investigate invented financial updates from a tool sold to simplify their lives.

Our editorial recommendation is to avoid Muse. We do not think the convenience justifies this uncertainty about access and behavior. If you already use it, review and revoke its permissions and connected accounts.

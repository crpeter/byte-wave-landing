Why We Need Tools to Check AI-Generated Photos and Videos

By ByteWave · Published September 19, 2026 · Updated September 19, 2026

Published by ByteWave, which makes the app discussed here. AI tools assisted research, drafting, and editing.

Making synthetic media easy to create should come with a corresponding investment in helping people understand what they receive. We believe tools for inspecting media should be accessible, respect privacy, and explain their evidence clearly. That is part of what we are building in ByteWave.

People need evidence, not another confident answer

Imagine receiving a clip presented as footage of an event yesterday. There are two questions: how was the file made, and does it support the story attached to it? A tool that addresses only the first cannot settle the second. Even a camera recording could be old footage shared with a new caption.

That is why we want checking tools to complement source research. The original creator, date, context, and corroborating evidence still matter. A useful interface should help someone understand the evidence they have, without pretending to resolve questions it cannot answer.

What C2PA Content Credentials can tell you

C2PA Content Credentials carry signed statements about a file's origin and editing history, which can include AI use. Validation checks the credential's integrity and connection to the asset; it does not certify that a scene happened. Credentials are optional, so their absence is not proof of either camera capture or AI generation. Source: the C2PA explainer.

A useful result answers a specific question: what does the signed record disclose, and did it pass the relevant checks? “Has credentials” is too broad a label to answer whether generative AI was used.

Why an AI detector and a credential reader are different

A detector's classification is an assessment. A credential reader examines recorded claims and their verification status. When choosing a tool, ask which kind of result it provides, whether its score is calibrated, and what it reports when evidence is missing. Do not convert a percentage into a certainty the tool has not established.

People need accessible ways to inspect media

Our position is straightforward: if tools make it easy to create synthetic media, people should also have accessible ways to examine the evidence attached to it. That should include the person receiving a family message, not just a newsroom or a forensic specialist.

A useful checking tool should make three things clear: what it inspected, what evidence supports its result, and what remains unknown. An inconclusive result is useful information. Hiding uncertainty behind a dramatic “real” or “fake” label makes the reader's next decision harder.

Privacy belongs in that discussion too. Before sending a personal image to a checking service, ask whether uploading is necessary, what the service stores, and whether the file may be used for training. Choose a workflow whose data handling you understand.

What if sharing removed the credentials?

C2PA documents that credentials can be separated from media. Some implementations can rediscover them using supported watermarks or fingerprints, but that capability is not universal. An ordinary embedded-credential check should not be mistaken for a search of every possible external record. Source: C2PA's explanation of stripped metadata.

This is a design challenge for checking tools: missing evidence needs an honest explanation. A file should not receive a reassuring verdict merely because the information needed to assess it is unavailable.

Europe’s Tech Dependence Is Everyone’s Problem.

By ByteWave · Published October 18, 2026

AI disclosure: This article includes text generated with an AI language model (LLM). Published by ByteWave. Sources are linked below; the conclusions are our opinion.

A server address does not tell you who holds the off switch.

Europe is asking who controls the tools.

On June 3, 2026, the European Commission presented a tech sovereignty package aimed at reducing dependence on outside suppliers. It included proposals for the Cloud and AI Development Act and Chips Act 2.0, alongside an Open Source Strategy.

These were legislative proposals, not proof that Europe had already replaced its technology suppliers. The Commission’s stated goal is greater control over key technologies, data, and infrastructure.

You do not need the jargon.

The everyday version is simple: can you keep working if your supplier changes the terms, suspends your account, or leaves your market?

In our view, the useful questions go beyond where a server sits. Who operates the service? Who controls your account? Can you move your files? Can you switch tools without starting again?

A European provider can still lock you in. A non-European tool can still offer useful local operation and portable files. Judge the actual dependencies, not just the flag on the homepage.

Keep an exit you can actually use.

For creators, that starts with original footage stored locally, separate backups, and finished videos you can play outside the editor that made them.

ByteWave’s contribution is modest and practical: local editing and export without an account. Publishing remains an online service. We still depend on Apple’s platforms; we are not claiming complete independence.

Choosing a tool should not mean handing over every route back to your own work.

Keep the originals. Keep the exports. Test the exit.

Start with the offline editing test. For a concrete example of access changing across borders, read Adobe’s 2019 Venezuela restriction.

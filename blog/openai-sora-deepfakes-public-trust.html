OpenAI made convincing video easier to generate. Protecting the people watching should matter just as much.

Sora 2 generated video with synchronized audio. That technology supported creative work, but could also make a fabricated scene look recorded. This is a historical example: OpenAI says the Sora web and app experiences ended April 26, 2026, and named September 24, 2026 as the API end date.

Our concern is OpenAI’s responsibility for that power. Someone scrolling past a clip should not have to become a forensic analyst to decide whether to believe it.

Your face should not be someone else’s content

In October 2025, TIME reported that deepfake-detection company Reality Defender said it had bypassed Sora’s impersonation safeguards using public footage of CEOs and entertainers.

OpenAI told TIME the researchers had built a sophisticated deepfake system and said it was strengthening the protections. This was a reported test at that time, not proof that the same bypass works today.

But it illustrates the stakes: the person being impersonated may never have agreed to participate. Consent needs to survive determined misuse.

Credit the safeguards. Question their limits.

OpenAI has documented Content Credentials, watermarking, and public verification tools. Those are useful steps. It also acknowledges that metadata can be lost or removed, and that missing signals cannot settle a file’s origin.

Our standard is simple: clear disclosure, meaningful consent, independent testing, and ways for ordinary people to check what reaches them. OpenAI should earn trust through evidence that those protections work.

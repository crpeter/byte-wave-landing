Being good at building software does not entitle you to govern the people who use it. However intelligent the software gets.

What Tegmark alleged

In a November 27 report by Frank Landymore at Futurism, MIT physicist and AI safety advocate Max Tegmark is quoted describing conversations with Anthropic researchers after drinks. He alleges that those conversations turn toward replacing the US government and taking over the world, and interprets the phrase alignment without control as language for that ambition.

Futurism links to a Novara Media interview. This is Tegmark’s account and interpretation, as reported by Futurism. The report does not establish an Anthropic plan to overthrow a government, and ByteWave has not independently verified the private conversations. It includes no response from Anthropic to this allegation.

That distinction matters. So does the question underneath it: who gets to decide how much power an AI system should have over everyone else?

A company cannot appoint itself humanity’s guardian

Our objection is straightforward. A promise that a powerful system will act in our interests cannot substitute for our ability to challenge its decisions, limit its authority, or refuse it altogether.

Imagine a company telling a town that its new decision system is wiser than the residents. It promises fair outcomes. But the residents cannot remove it, change its remit, or appeal to anyone with the power to overrule it. Better predictions would not fix that arrangement. The missing ingredient is consent.

Calling a system aligned leaves the most consequential questions open. Aligned with whose values? Chosen through what process? Revised by whom when people disagree?

Anthropic’s own research makes the point

In its October 2023 Collective Constitutional AI experiment, Anthropic and the Collective Intelligence Project asked roughly 1,000 Americans to help choose principles for an AI system. Anthropic acknowledged the substantial influence developers have when they select those principles themselves.

The resulting public constitution overlapped with the company-written version by roughly 50% in concepts and values. Anthropic also described subjective decisions involved in translating public input into training principles, and acknowledged that the sample was not globally representative.

That experiment deserves credit. It demonstrates an effort to involve people outside the company, and it complicates any blanket claim that Anthropic has no interest in public participation. It does not corroborate Tegmark’s allegation.

It also illustrates why consultation is only a beginning. Asking people what they want a chatbot to do does not authorize that chatbot, or its owner, to rule them. Public input needs to lead to power people can actually exercise.

Useful software should leave people in charge

We want enforceable limits, independent scrutiny, meaningful ways to contest consequential decisions, and accountable people who can stop a system when it causes harm. Neither a corporate board nor an unaccountable government should get a blank check to impose it.

That principle applies at the smaller scale where ByteWave works, too. We build a video editor. Local editing runs on your device without an account; online publishing is optional. Those choices do not solve AI governance. They give the person making a video practical control over a task they came to do.

We use AI assistance, including for this article, as the disclosure above states. Using a tool does not oblige anyone to endorse its maker’s ambitions or surrender judgment to its output.

Build something useful. Let people decide whether they want it. Nobody elected your machine god.

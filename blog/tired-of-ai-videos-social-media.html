Tired of AI Videos on Social Media? Take Back Creative Control

By ByteWave · Published September 12, 2026 · Sources checked September 12, 2026

Published by ByteWave, which makes the app discussed here. AI tools assisted research, drafting, and editing. Platform guidance is based on the linked documentation; feature availability can vary.

You opened a feed to see what people were doing. Instead, you are trying to work out whether the animal, vacation, performance, or entire person in a video exists. If that is wearing you out, wanting more control over what you watch and create is a reasonable response.

At ByteWave, we think video tools should leave room for the footage you went out and recorded. The shot you waited for, the sound you captured, and the edit you chose are worth building around.

People often call repetitive, unwanted generated content “AI slop.” That frustration can involve both the content and the feed that keeps recommending it. Changing creation tools and changing viewing preferences solve different parts of the experience.

Which companies are promoting AI video?

The shift extends beyond Facebook. The examples below describe documented products and integrations as checked September 12, 2026. They do not establish which tool made any particular clip in your feed.

Meta: Facebook, Instagram, and Vibes
Meta's Vibes announcement introduced a feed for generated videos and remixing, with sharing to Facebook and Instagram Stories and Reels. Generating and distributing clips are linked parts of that product.
ByteDance's video ecosystem: TikTok, CapCut, Dreamina, and Pippit
CapCut markets AI video generation alongside editing. Dreamina promotes generated images, video, and avatars; Pippit promotes automated video creation for marketing. TikTok also describes its own AI creation and labeling efforts in its AI-content announcement.
Google: Gemini, Veo, and Flow
Google introduced Flow as a filmmaking tool built around Veo, Imagen, and Gemini, including a showcase of generated clips. This is a direct push into creating scenes from generative models.
OpenAI: Sora and its discontinuation
OpenAI launched Sora as a video-generation product with a social app. Its current discontinuation notice says the web and app experiences ended April 26, 2026, and the API is scheduled to end September 24, 2026. Sora belongs in the history of this push, rather than a recommendation to download an active social app.
Adobe, Runway, Grok Imagine, Luma, and Pika
Adobe Firefly, Runway, Grok Imagine, Luma, and Pika offer video-generation tools. Their workflows and terms differ; being a generator does not mean each operates a Facebook-style social feed.
Anthropic's Claude and other assistants connected to video tools
A general assistant can also be part of a generation workflow through another provider. For example, Pika advertises integration support for Claude Code, Codex, and ChatGPT. That is evidence of third-party access through those assistants, not evidence that Anthropic makes the underlying video model or runs an AI-video feed.

Our objection is to treating an endless supply of generated clips as something everyone ought to want. A useful tool should let you decide when it belongs in your work and what you want in your feed.

What should a social-media alternative promise?

Look for clear answers about feed controls, account requirements, uploads, and whether personalization stores a history of your viewing. ByteWave documents anonymous browsing and optional local personalization without server-side watch history. Published videos and optional profiles still use online services.

A privacy architecture alone cannot guarantee that every uploaded video is camera-recorded. Neither can an export signature: provenance helps establish file origin and changes, not whether the depicted event happened. Those are the boundaries to understand when choosing where to watch and share.

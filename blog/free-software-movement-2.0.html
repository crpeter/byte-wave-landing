Free Software Movement 2.0

July 2026 · 4 min read
This post reflects ByteWave's editorial position. Written with LLM assistance.

In the 1980s, Richard Stallman looked at what was happening to software and said: this is a moral problem. Companies were locking down source code, restricting what users could do with the tools they depended on, and treating control over software as a business model. The free software movement was his answer. Software should respect the people who use it. Users deserve freedom.

That movement changed everything. Linux, Firefox, Android, the entire open-source ecosystem that modern tech runs on exists because of it. But the threat to users has evolved, and the definition of freedom needs to evolve with it.

"Free" doesn't mean what it used to

Today the word free has been gutted. Every app in the App Store says it's free. What they actually mean is: you don't pay with money. You pay with your data, your attention, your behavioral patterns, your location history, your face, your contacts. The product is free because you are the product.

This isn't a secret. Everyone knows it. But knowing it and doing something about it are different things.

What we mean by free

You don't pay for the app. Not now, not ever. There is no premium tier. There is no paywall hiding the good features behind a subscription.

We don't collect your data. The App Store privacy label says "Data Not Collected." That's not a technicality or a marketing angle. It's a foundational architecture decision. Video processing happens on your device. There is no analytics SDK watching what you do. We chose this knowing it means we can't run targeted ads or build engagement models or do any of the things that make apps "sticky" in the way investors like to hear about.

Revenue comes from affiliate partnerships and house ads, never from selling access to our users. And a portion of that revenue goes to charitable causes through ByteWave Causes, where users signal which causes matter most to them.

We geo-block countries that financially support Russia's war in Ukraine. That means walking away from potential markets on principle.

None of this is accidental. Every one of these decisions costs us something. That's how you know they're real.

Why "2.0"

The original free software movement was about source code. Access to it, the right to modify it, the right to distribute it. Those freedoms still matter. ByteWave is not open source, and we're not pretending otherwise.

But the biggest threat to users in 2026 is not locked source code. It's the architecture of extraction that the entire app economy is built on. Free-to-download apps that monetize through surveillance. Social platforms that optimize for addiction because engagement drives ad revenue. Companies that collect everything, store it forever, and fight regulation at every turn.

The freedoms that matter now are different:

Freedom from surveillance as a business model. Freedom from paywalls that hold your own creativity hostage. Freedom from platforms that treat your attention as inventory to be sold. Freedom to use powerful tools without compromising your privacy or your values.

That's what we're building toward. Not just a video editor. A proof of concept that software can be genuinely free, self-sustaining, and built around the interests of the people who use it.

This is just the start

ByteWave is one app made by one person. We're not pretending to have solved anything yet. But we think the model matters. Privacy-first, permanently free, values-driven, with revenue flowing toward causes instead of just toward growth.

If that idea works here, it can work elsewhere. And if enough people build software this way, the definition of "free" starts to mean something again.

Free means free

No paywalls. No data collection. No watermarks. Just a video editor that respects you.

Take the chatbot away. Can the kid still do the math?

In an experiment with nearly 1,000 high school students, the group with access to a standard AI chatbot scored 48% higher on practice problems than classmates who worked without it. On an exam without the chatbot, they scored 17% lower than those same classmates. The homework looked better. The kids who did their own practice knew more when it counted.

The telling detail: a version designed to give hints instead of handing over solutions largely avoided that penalty. There’s a difference between helping a kid take the next step and taking it for them.

Google should pay attention. In separate experiments, people who learned about a topic from AI summaries later gave thinner, less original advice than people who followed links and read the sources. That held even when the underlying facts were the same. A wrong summary can mislead a kid. A correct one can do the very work the kid came to school to learn.

Google should keep AI summaries out of school searches. Let students open the sources, spot the contradiction, and decide whose evidence holds up. That judgment belongs to the kid. Google has no business doing it for them.

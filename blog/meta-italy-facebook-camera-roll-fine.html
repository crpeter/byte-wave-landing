Italy has fined Meta €805 million ($887 million) over Facebook's access to Android users’ photo and video galleries. The issue wasn't simply whether someone tapped Allow. It was whether they understood what they were allowing.

What Italy's regulator found

On November 14, 2026, Italy’s data protection authority, the Garante, announced an €805 million fine against Meta Platforms Ireland Limited. It found that Facebook's Android app accessed photos, videos, and associated metadata stored on Italian users' phones without obtaining sufficiently specific, informed consent.

The decision centers on a feature available since 2023: Get suggestions from gallery when browsing Facebook. It surfaced items from a person's camera roll as suggestions for posts. The authority said its investigation concerned roughly 20 million Android users in Italy.

That number describes the scale of the affected user population. It does not establish that Meta copied every photo from every one of those phones.

A permission prompt is not a blank check

The Garante found that gallery access was sought through a single Android permission used for multiple purposes. Facebook preceded it with a generic notice about enabling additional features; the gallery-suggestions function was also enabled by default. Full photo-library permission could give the app ongoing access to the entire archive, while users and the regulator could not determine precisely which files the app actually used.

The authority said this violated Article 122 of Italy's Privacy Code, which implements the European ePrivacy framework. Its position is important: valid consent matters even when data isn't uploaded or a specific injury cannot be shown. A phone's camera roll is part of someone's private life, not a pool of content for an app to sample whenever it finds a use for it.

Meta disputes the ruling

Meta says it will appeal. In a statement reported by Reuters, a company spokesperson rejected the regulator’s assessment and called the penalty disproportionate. The company emphasized that people must first grant device permission and can later change it in their settings.

That is Meta's argument, and it deserves to be stated accurately. But it doesn't answer the narrower question raised by the Garante: did people receive a clear, separate explanation of what the gallery-suggestions feature would do before access was granted?

What happens next

The Garante ordered Meta to obtain consent through a distinct, specific request and to keep the feature off by default, including for people who previously granted broad permission. The company was given 60 days to meet those requirements, with earlier and later reporting deadlines. Its planned appeal means the dispute is not necessarily over.

Crucially, the investigation did not decide whether gallery contents were used to train AI. Meta denies doing that. The authority said a newer, 2026 feature allowing cloud processing of gallery content may be examined separately.

That distinction matters alongside our earlier article on Facebook's camera-roll suggestions. Meta has described a newer 2026 rollout as opt-in; Italy's ruling concerns an earlier Android gallery-suggestions feature that the regulator found was on by default. They should not be conflated.

Your photos should stay yours

If you use Facebook on Android, review its access to photos and videos in your phone's app-permission settings. Check Facebook's own gallery-suggestion settings where available. Menu names vary by Android version and Facebook app version; restricting access may affect features that need to open your library.

ByteWave takes a different approach: edit your own footage locally on your Apple device, without an account for local editing, then decide whether to share it. The point isn't that every useful suggestion is bad. It's that a company's convenience should never replace your informed choice.

Permission to access your camera roll should mean what you think it means.
